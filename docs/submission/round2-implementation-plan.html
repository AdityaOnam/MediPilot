01What changed, and why

Every row below is a place where the Round 1 plan said something the Round 2 brief or the design session contradicts. Nothing else in the original plan is discarded — but these are the edits that have to land before anything else is worth building.

02The submission gate

These are not aspirations. They are the stated minimum prototype expectations, and each one is a demonstrable behaviour with an owner and a demo moment. If a feature elsewhere in this plan competes for time with one of these, the item below wins.

  • Triage scoring on 15–20 simulated patient records Our corpus is 20 (§14), sized so every required edge case gets its own record rather than being bundled.
  • At least one ambiguous presentation P-07 — abdominal pain that is either gastritis or an inferior MI, with the deciding evidence deliberately absent at T0.
  • At least one pediatric or geriatric case Both. P-03 is a 3-year-old at 38.5 °C; P-04 is a 75-year-old at the same temperature. Same number, opposite dispositions — this is the age-stratification demo.
  • At least one zero-history first-time patient P-11 — no prior record, no ABHA link, nothing but what is observed in the room.
  • Behaviour under a simulated 3× surge A scripted run at 3× arrival rate showing which cadences stretch, which hold, and the queue re-ordering as it happens (§11).
  • Uncertainty surfaced explicitly — never a score without a confidence indicator Enforced at the output contract, not by UI convention: a score object without a populated confidence field fails validation and is not renderable.
  • At least one clinician override, with the log shown P-14 — nurse overrides a Yellow to Red on physical findings the system could not see. The demo shows the resulting record field by field (§13).
One more, self-imposed

The brief says teams must demonstrate the escalation bias explicitly in their prototype. A claim in the deck does not satisfy that. We show it as a live control: move the cost ratio R and watch the threshold and the resulting board change, with the same patients re-sorting in front of the judges.

The brief asks for these to be stated clearly rather than left implicit.

Why three tiers and not five

The brief allows either. We choose the three-tier Red/Yellow/Green AIIMS Triage Protocol for three reasons, and the choice needs defending because a five-level scale looks more sophisticated on a slide.

  • It is the deployed Indian standard. ATP is in use at AIIMS Bhubaneswar, CMC Vellore, GTB Delhi and the Kerala DHS. A tool that speaks a different vocabulary than the ward it sits in gets worked around.
  • Fewer boundaries means fewer places to be wrong. A five-level scale has four decision boundaries; three tiers has two. With mistriage already running near a third of visits under ESI, adding boundaries adds error surface.
  • The colour is not the output. The band is a queue position; the card carries the reasoning and the confidence. Granularity lives in the probability and the acuity set, not in the number of buckets.

We keep an internal five-point mapping so the system can export to ESI-speaking hospitals without re-training.

04Design invariants

Six rules that no module may violate and no configuration may relax. Everything else in this document is a design decision; these are constraints. Each maps to a test in the prototype suite so a violation fails the build rather than being caught in review.

Invariant 1 — Asymmetric autonomy

The system may raise a patient's band on its own. It may never lower a patient below a human-assigned band on its own. Escalation costs one assessment; de-escalation can cost a life.

Invariant 2 — No naked scores

Every emitted risk value carries a confidence indicator and the inputs it was computed from. A score object missing either fails schema validation and cannot reach a screen.

Invariant 3 — Age is never assumed

No threshold, weight or calibration is applied without an age stratum resolved first. Where age is unknown, the system uses the widest-safety stratum and says that it did.

Invariant 4 — Freshness is part of the value

A measurement is a tuple of value, timestamp, source and validity. A reading older than twice its band's cadence is discounted; older than three times, it is treated as missing.

Invariant 5 — Abstention is loud, and never Green

When the system cannot score a patient it says so and routes to a human. An abstained patient with no human assessment yet holds at Yellow, never Green.

Invariant 6 — The human closes every loop

Final clinical action belongs to licensed staff. Override is always available, always one touch, and always recorded with a reason.

06Age stratification

The brief is unusually direct here: applying one adult-calibrated scoring model across all age groups introduces silent safety risk. Silent is the operative word — the model returns a confident number and nothing looks wrong. This is the single largest gap in the Round 1 plan.

Two mechanisms, deliberately separate. Thresholds change what counts as abnormal. Calibration changes how a given abnormality maps to risk. Conflating them produces a model that is right about the vitals and wrong about the danger.

Child1 – 12 y70–14018–30Compensates well then decompensates abruptly — trend beats snapshot.

The demo case, built deliberately

P-03 and P-04 both present at 38.5 °C. The 3-year-old with tachypnoea and poor feeding escalates; the 75-year-old with the same temperature and a normal-looking heart rate also escalates, because in the geriatric stratum an unremarkable pulse alongside fever is a weaker negative than it is at 30. A single adult model would have sorted at least one of them wrong, and would have looked confident doing it.

Where age is unknown

Unaccompanied and unresponsive patients arrive without a stated age. The system estimates a coarse stratum from what is available, applies the widest-safety configuration across the plausible range, marks the stratum as inferred, and puts that on the card. An inferred stratum is a standing reason for reduced confidence — it never silently resolves.

07Self-report reliability

The brief notes that patients under-report pain and symptoms, and that presentation varies by age and condition. The session notes reached the same place from the other direction — a shorthand about discounting information from older patients.

A deliberate rewording

The session shorthand — "for ≥ 60, consider information unreliable" — is implemented, but not as written. Encoded literally it is both indefensible and wrong in direction: it would let the system discount a 70-year-old reporting chest pain. The clinically correct and defensible form is asymmetric, and it is the form we build.

The rule

Reliability weighting reduces the evidential weight of a reassuring answer. It never reduces the weight of an alarming one. "No chest pain" from a patient in a low-reliability context is weak evidence of absence; "chest pain" from the same patient is full-strength evidence of presence.

In model terms: these factors lower the negative predictive value of self-report. They do not touch the positive predictive value. Practically, a reassuring answer under a reliability discount widens the conformal set rather than lowering the score — which pushes toward abstention and a human look, not toward Green.

Factors that lower reassurance weight

  • Geriatric stratum — atypical presentation is well documented: silent MI, afebrile sepsis, shock presenting as confusion or as abdominal pain.
  • Communication barrier — the interaction language is not the patient's first language, or the exchange is running through an attendant rather than the patient.
  • Health-literacy signal — the patient's answers to the tree indicate unfamiliarity with the terms being used. Inferred from the interaction itself, never from an assumption about a demographic.
  • Stoic-presentation flag — the nurse can set this in one tap when a patient's manner and their physiology disagree. Clinician-set, not model-guessed.
  • Non-assisted arrival — nobody present to corroborate or contradict the history.
  • Analgesia already given — pre-hospital pain relief masks the pain score outright.

What this is not

No factor here is a protected attribute used as a direct model input. Age enters through the stratum, which is clinical. Language and literacy enter through observed interaction, not through inferred background. Every applied discount appears on the nurse's card by name, so a clinician can see and reject the reasoning — which is also how we detect it going wrong.

08Recheck cadence — two clocks

Round 1 promised continuous re-triage every five minutes for everyone. The session notes said Red 5, Yellow 30, Green 60. These are not in conflict once you separate the two things a "recheck" can mean, and separating them is the more defensible design than either alone.

  • Re-scoring is the model running again over whatever data exists. It costs milliseconds of edge compute. There is no reason to ration it — every waiting patient is re-scored every 5 minutes regardless of band.
  • Re-measurement is a human or a station physically taking a fresh set of vitals. It costs the scarcest resource in the department. It is rationed by band.

Re-scoring stale data does not create information, which is exactly why the freshness contract (Invariant 4) matters: as a reading ages past its band's cadence, confidence decays and the patient surfaces for re-measurement on that basis rather than on a fixed timer alone.

BandRe-scoreRe-measureWait ceilingOn breach of ceiling
Red 60 s5 min0 min Should not be waiting at all. Any Red still queued at 5 min pages the senior clinician and is flagged on the board.
Yellow 5 min30 min60 min Forced re-measurement task raised. If it cannot be completed within 15 min, the patient escalates to Red on time-in-queue alone.
Green 5 min60 min120 min Forced re-measurement and a wellbeing contact. Two consecutive missed rechecks escalate to Yellow.
Abstained 5 minon review15 min Holds at Yellow floor (Invariant 5). Unreviewed past 15 min, it is raised as an unmet-review breach, not left in the queue.

The wait ceiling is the brief's requirement made concrete: re-assessment triggers when wait time exceeds the safe threshold for that severity level, independently of whether any vital has changed.

Who performs the recheck

The session notes raised this and it turns out to matter twice — once for whether the recheck happens at all, and once for how much the resulting number is trusted.

Recheck station — full trust

Instrumented bay, device-reported, timestamped at source. The only path that produces a full-confidence reading. Red rechecks use this or a nurse; nothing else.

Nurse or attendant — full trust, rationed

Manual entry by trained staff. Trusted fully, but competes with everything else the nurse is doing, which is why the router tracks deadline breaches rather than assuming completion.

Accompanying family — partial trust

Can answer wellbeing prompts and report visible change on a Green patient. Cannot close a Yellow or Red recheck. Their input raises a flag for staff; it never satisfies the requirement itself.

Patient self-service — signal only

The patient's own "I feel worse" is treated as a symptom report, not a measurement. It can escalate; it can never satisfy a recheck or de-escalate.

09Assisted and non-assisted intake

Round 1 treated the unattended patient as an assistance button. The session made it an intake branch, which is correct: whether anyone is with the patient changes what the system can ask, what it can trust, and what it must do when the patient stops responding.

The sequence

  1. Is anyone with you? Asked plainly, answered by tap or voice. Recorded as a field, because it feeds reliability weighting (§7) and the recheck routing (§8).
  2. Would you prefer a person? The offer of human assistance comes before the system proceeds on its own, not after it fails. A patient who wants a person gets queued for one and the machine stops asking clinical questions.
  3. Consent to use your medical information. A yes/no gate, in the patient's language, distinct from consent to be treated. Declining is a supported state — the patient is still triaged, on observed and measured data only.
  4. Proceed, with the branch recorded. Everything downstream knows which path produced the data.
Declining consent must not be a penalty

A patient who declines to share history is triaged on vitals and observation. They may end up with a wider conformal set and therefore a more cautious band — which under Invariant 1 means more attention, not less. The system must never present declining as a reason for a worse queue position, and the card must not display the refusal as a risk factor.

The non-clinical assistance path

Distinct from everything above and deliberately kept out of the risk model. A patient calling for help with water, a toilet, a translator or a relative generates a staff task, not a risk signal. Conflating the two would train the model that asking for help predicts deterioration, and would teach patients that pressing the button re-triages them.

One exception, rule-based rather than learned: repeated assistance calls from the same patient within a short window raise a wellbeing check. Someone pressing a button four times in ten minutes is telling the department something.

10The immediate red-flag pass

From the session notes: some presentations should never wait for a model. If a delay could cost a life, the patient is Red now, and the reasoning can catch up afterwards.

This runs on the structured output of the intake conversation, before the risk model is consulted, in parallel with the vitals rule engine. It is deterministic, it is auditable, and it is short enough that a clinician can read the whole thing.

The design constraint this had to satisfy

Implemented naively — "ask the model whether any delay might cost this person's life" — this is an LLM assigning acuity, which the architecture prohibits and which the literature says it does badly, with a documented bias toward overestimating severity. The prohibition is worth keeping, so the mechanism is split: the LLM extracts observations; a fixed table maps observations to Red. The model reports what was said. It does not decide what it means.

A red-flag Red is not overridable downward by the system under any subsequent model output — only a clinician can move it, with a reason. The flag also displays as the leading factor on the card, so nobody is left wondering why a patient with unremarkable vitals went to the front.

11Surge behaviour

The brief asks how the system behaves differently during a surge versus a quiet shift, and the prototype must demonstrate 3× volume. This is separate from mass-casualty mode: a surge is a busy Tuesday, and the system enters it on its own. Mass casualty changes the ethical objective and stays behind an authenticated senior clinician action.

At 3× our design point that is roughly 1,500 arrivals a day, one a minute, with 40–80 patients waiting concurrently and possibly 40 patients per nurse. The compute is untroubled. The humans are the constrained resource, so surge policy is entirely about how the system spends their attention.

Under 3× loadBehaviour
Red re-measurementHolds at 5 min — never stretches. This is the one cadence surge does not touch.
Yellow re-measurementStretches 30 → 45 min, and the wait ceiling tightens rather than loosens, so time-in-queue does more of the work.
Green re-measurementStretches 60 → 90 min, with wellbeing contact taking over part of the load.
AlertsRanked, never suppressed. A capped feed sorted by risk × time-waiting, so a fatigued nurse sees the worst first instead of a wall.
AbstentionsStill abstain — but the abstention queue is itself prioritised, because under surge "wait for a nurse" is a longer sentence than usual.
Nurse interfaceSwitches from card-detail to board-density: more patients visible, reasoning one tap away rather than on screen.
RoutingBed and roster feeds carry more weight in the soft dimension. Hard clinical rules are untouched.
What surge mode is forbidden from doing

It may not raise R to reduce alarm volume. It may not resolve abstentions by guessing. It may not de-escalate anyone to free capacity. Every one of those makes the board look calmer while making the department more dangerous, and each is the obvious temptation under load — so each is blocked in code, not in policy.

Surge entry and exit are logged with their triggers, so the post-hoc question "why did the cadence change at 14:20" has an answer.

12Explainability, three channels

Structure carried over from Round 1, with the third channel re-scoped. The brief's constraint is severe — the explanation has to be absorbed in seconds, by someone already managing several patients — so the card shows three things and hides the rest behind a tap.

Channel 1 — What drove this

The two strongest contributing factors, with direction and magnitude, plus one factor arguing the other way. The opposing factor is deliberate: it gives the clinician something to falsify, which is the practical defence against nodding along.

Channel 2 — What was considered and did not move it

Normal and weakly-contributing parameters, so the nurse does not assume every available number was decisive. This is also where an applied reliability discount is named (§7).

Channel 3 — What was said, and what happened since

Re-scoped from a generic event log. It now separates the narrative contribution — the phrases the LLM extracted and what they triggered — from the timeline of band changes, alarms, stale readings, missed rechecks and overrides. Narrative evidence is the least familiar input a nurse will encounter, so it gets its own space rather than being folded into a feature list.

Every card also carries the confidence indicator (Invariant 2) and, where relevant, the reason confidence is low: missing field, stale reading, inferred age stratum, disagreeing sensors, or a patient unlike the local distribution.

14Data strategy and the synthetic corpus

The honest position: we have no real Indian ED dataset in hand, and we will not have one before the Round 2 deadline. Public sources (MIMIC-IV-ED and similar) are US populations and are useful for architecture validation but cannot support a claim about Indian EDs. Everything the judges see runs on synthetic data, and the brief explicitly permits this — realistic, even if simplified or simulated.

That makes the generator the critical path. It is the first thing built, because nothing else can be tested until it exists.

Generating data that is not just noise

Sampling SpO₂, blood pressure and heart rate independently produces combinations no human has ever presented with, and a model trained on them learns nothing transferable. The generator is therefore built around a small number of clinical trajectories rather than around marginal distributions.

  • Condition first, vitals second. Pick a presentation, then sample vitals from that presentation's joint distribution, conditioned on age stratum.
  • Trajectories, not snapshots. Each patient has a time series with a physiologically plausible path — compensation then decompensation, or gradual improvement — so the trend head has something real to learn.
  • Correlations preserved. Rising heart rate with falling blood pressure; respiratory rate tracking hypoxia; fever with tachycardia at a stratum-appropriate ratio.
  • Missingness modelled, not random. Missing data in an ED is structured — the zero-history patient, the failed sensor, the reading nobody had time to take. Each gets its own mechanism.
  • Measurement error and device bias. Including the pulse-oximeter bias case, which is one of the few places a plausible-looking normal reading is actively dangerous.

Validating it before trusting it

  • Statistical — marginals, correlation structure and missingness patterns compared against published ED reference distributions.
  • Clinical — a clinician reviews a sample and answers one question: could this patient exist? Cheap, and it catches what the statistics miss.
  • Temporal — trajectories checked for physiological plausibility over time, not only at each instant.
  • Transfer — train on synthetic, test on the public real-data benchmark. Degradation is expected; the size of it is the number we report rather than hide.

Stack

Chosen to be defensible under questioning and buildable in the time available. The session flagged LLM and speech-transformer integration as an open feasibility question (§16); the stack below keeps that on a path where failure is survivable.

  • Risk model — gradient-boosted trees on tabular features, plus a small sequence model for the trend head. Fast, interpretable, handles missing values natively, and trains on a laptop.
  • Narrative — a small instruction-following model with constrained decoding to a fixed schema. Constrained decoding is what makes the perception-only guarantee real rather than aspirational.
  • Speech — Whisper-class ASR, quantised, evaluated on Indian-accented and code-mixed speech before we depend on it.
  • Backend — a single service holding the pipeline, scheduler and audit log. Simulated clock so a 3-hour ED shift demos in three minutes.
  • Frontend — the nurse board and card, the patient intake flow, and a judge-facing control panel exposing R, surge rate and simulation speed.

Sequence

  1. Generator and corpus. Nothing downstream is testable without it. Ends when the 20 records exist and pass clinical review.
  2. Pipeline spine. Ingestion → validation → freshness → model → threshold → band. Headless, tested against the corpus.
  3. Safety layers. Age stratification, red-flag pass, emergency rules, abstention gate. The invariant tests land here.
  4. Scheduler. Two clocks, wait ceilings, breach escalation, recheck ownership.
  5. Interface. Board, card, override capture, audit rendering.
  6. Surge controller. Cheapest step once the scheduler exists, and it exercises everything before it.
  7. Intake conversation. Question tree, then voice, then additional languages. Staged so the demo survives the speech layer not landing.
  8. Demo rehearsal. Treated as a build step, not an afterthought.

Demo narrative

The story is one patient, then the system around them. P-05 arrives with mild chest discomfort and is correctly assigned Yellow — the nurse did nothing wrong. Eighteen minutes later, with the nurse registering three new arrivals, the queue re-orders itself and P-05 moves to the front, with the reasoning visible and one factor arguing against. Then: the same department at 3× load, and what the system refuses to do to cope. Then: a nurse overriding it, and exactly what that leaves behind in the record.

16Open risks

Carried forward from the session, stated plainly so they are tracked rather than discovered.

RiskExposureResponse
No real dataset High — everything shown is synthetic. Say so, first and clearly. Report the synthetic-to-real transfer gap as a number. A team that states this openly is more credible than one that lets a judge find it.
Speech integration slips Medium — ASR on code-mixed Indian speech is genuinely hard. Sequenced last with a typed fallback that is complete on its own. The demo must not depend on it.
Constrained decoding underperforms Medium — schema-locked extraction can be brittle on messy input. Evaluate early on deliberately messy transcripts. The red-flag table is the safety net: it runs on whatever fields were extracted, and missing fields mean abstention, not silence.
Clinical review capacity Medium — the corpus, the red-flag list and the age bands all need a clinician's eye. Bundle everything needing review into one package and one sitting. Identify the reviewer now, not when the package is ready.
Demo breadth beats demo depth Medium — the standard failure mode. The scope table in §15 is a commitment. Anything not in the left column is described, not built, and saying so out loud is a strength.
Frontend polish absorbs the schedule Low but real — the session wanted heavy animation. The interface must be legible, fast and honest. Motion earns its place only where it shows a queue re-ordering, which is the one thing worth animating.
Routing is combinatorial Low at prototype scale. Constrained assignment is hard in general; at 80 patients a greedy assignment under hard rules is optimal enough. Note the relaxation rather than pretending it is exact.
